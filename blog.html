01 · Background

Why synthesizability matters

Drug discovery is expensive and slow. Getting a single compound from early-stage discovery to an approved therapy typically takes over a decade and costs around $2 billion. A big part of the problem is the sheer size of chemical space — the number of possible drug-like molecules is estimated at around 1060, which makes any kind of exhaustive search completely impractical.

Generative models have become a popular approach for proposing new molecular candidates computationally. But most of these models focus almost entirely on predicted properties like drug-likeness or binding affinity, without asking a more basic question: can this molecule actually be made? A molecule with a great QED score is not very useful if synthesizing it would require 15 steps and reagents that cost thousands of dollars per gram.

Prior Work

The most directly relevant prior work is the SMILES VAE (Gómez-Bombarelli et al., 2018), which showed that training a VAE on SMILES strings gives a continuous latent space where gradient-based property optimization is possible. Our model builds directly on this. The VAE framework itself is from Kingma & Welling (2014), and we use cyclical KL annealing from Fu et al. (2019) to prevent posterior collapse during training. For tokenization, we follow Schwaller et al. (2019), splitting SMILES into chemically meaningful tokens rather than individual characters.

None of these works explicitly optimize for synthesis cost during generation — that is the gap we are trying to fill.

Our Approach: Adding a Cost Constraint

The SA score is a standard proxy for synthesis difficulty, ranging from 1 (easy) to 10 (practically impossible at scale). It is based on how frequently the substructures of a molecule appear in known compounds. Molecules built from common, purchasable fragments score lower. It is imperfect, but fast and widely used as a first-pass filter.

Our idea is simple: instead of optimizing only for drug-likeness, we add a penalty for high SA scores.

R(m) = QED(m) − λ · SAnorm(m)

SAnorm maps the SA score to [0, 1], and λ controls how much we care about synthesis cost relative to QED. A small λ means we mostly care about drug-likeness; a large λ means we really want the molecule to be makeable. By sweeping λ, we can trace out the trade-off between the two.

Core Hypothesis

Adding an explicit synthesis cost penalty (λ > 0) will reduce the mean SA score of generated molecules compared to unconstrained generation (λ = 0), at the cost of reduced novelty and QED — with this trade-off directly controllable by the magnitude of λ.

02 · Method

Model architecture & training

Dataset

We use the ZINC20-Druglike dataset, a curated collection of purchasable, drug-like small molecules represented as canonical SMILES strings. We use up to 500,000 molecules for training, with an 80/10/10 train/validation/test split determined by a fixed random seed. Downstream property scores (QED, SA) are computed on-the-fly using RDKit during training.

Tokenization

SMILES strings are tokenized using a regex-based approach following the Molecular Transformer convention (Schwaller et al., 2019). The tokenizer parses SMILES into chemically meaningful units — bracket atoms like [NH2], two-character elements like Br, single atoms, and bond/ring symbols — rather than splitting character-by-character. This produces a vocabulary of ~70 tokens.

Model Architecture

Encoder

Bidirectional LSTM

Maps token sequences to (μ, log σ²) in 256-dim latent space. Concatenates hidden + cell states from both directions.

Decoder

Unidirectional LSTM

Generates SMILES autoregressively, conditioned on z via learned linear projections into initial hidden/cell states.

Property Predictors ×2

MLP (256 → 128 → 1)

Predict QED and SA score from z. Enable differentiable reward computation in latent space during generation.

Latent Space

Gaussian, dim = 256

Reparameterization trick during training. Gradient ascent on z at inference time to maximize R(z).

Training Procedure

Phase 1 (Epochs 1–14): VAE trained with reconstruction loss (masked cross-entropy) plus KL divergence. Cyclical KL annealing (Fu et al., 2019) prevents posterior collapse by linearly ramping the KL weight over cycles of 10 epochs.

Phase 2 (Epochs 15–30): Property predictors trained jointly. At each batch, the decoder greedily decodes μ to SMILES, computes QED and SA via RDKit, and trains the predictors via MSE loss. VAE and predictors use separate Adam optimizers (lr = 3×10⁻⁴).

Constrained Generation

At generation time, we run 50 steps of Adam-based gradient ascent in latent space (lr = 0.01) with a small L2 anchor penalty (β = 0.01) to maximize the reward R(z). The decoder then samples from the optimized z to produce SMILES.

LSTM Baseline

A character-level LSTM language model trained directly on SMILES token sequences, without any latent space or property conditioning. Same architecture dimensions (embed=128, hidden=512, 2 layers), 30 epochs. This captures distributional statistics of the training data without any property optimization.

03 · Preliminary Results

Training & evaluation

Training Curves

Both models were trained for 30 epochs. VAE reconstruction loss dropped from ~1.1 to ~0.35. The KL loss started at ~0.2 at the beginning of each annealing cycle and came down close to 0 by the end, suggesting the cyclical annealing worked — the model did not collapse to ignoring the latent space. Validation loss stabilized around 0.57. The LSTM baseline converged similarly, with train and val loss both reaching ~0.57 and no obvious overfitting gap.

Generation Samples

200 molecules were generated per method. Sample outputs from the Constrained VAE (λ=0.5):

CO[C@H](CO)CNC(=O)Cn1cncn1
CC(=O)N[C@@H](C)C(=O)N[C@H](C)C(N)=O
C[S@](=O)C1CN(C(=O)CNCCO)C1

All parse successfully with RDKit and show features characteristic of drug-like fragments (amide bonds, stereocenters, heteroaromatic rings).

The results are directionally consistent with our hypothesis. The constrained VAE (λ=0.5) achieves the lowest mean SA score (3.33 vs. 3.38 for the unconstrained VAE), meaning it generates slightly easier-to-synthesize molecules. As expected, this comes with a small drop in QED (0.537 vs. 0.543). All three models achieve near-perfect validity and uniqueness. The differences at λ=0.5 are modest — we expect the effect to become more pronounced at higher λ values, which is the focus of our next experiments.

Next Two Experiments (Priority Order)

Full λ Sweep (λ ∈ {0.0, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0})

This is the core experiment. By running the ConstrainedGenerator across four λ values and reporting all six metrics, we can plot Pareto curves showing the trade-off between SA score and QED/novelty. Expected finding: SA score decreases monotonically with λ, while novelty and QED degrade slightly. If SA does not decrease with λ, it suggests the property predictors are not well calibrated — which is itself an informative result.

Property Predictor Calibration Check

Before trusting the λ sweep, we need to validate that our QED and SA predictors are accurate guides in latent space. We will encode a held-out set of molecules, run the predictors, and compare predicted vs. RDKit-computed scores. High correlation (R² > 0.7) is needed for gradient ascent to meaningfully steer generation.

Testing Different Economic Constraints

Beyond just varying λ, we want to explore alternative formulations of the economic constraint itself. For example, instead of using SA score as the cost proxy, we can try (1) a hard feasibility threshold that filters out any molecule with SA > 4, (2) replacing SA with a fragment-based purchasability score, or (3) combining SA with molecular weight as a joint cost term. This lets us test whether our results hold across different definitions of "cheap to make," and whether some constraint formulations produce a better quality-feasibility trade-off than others.

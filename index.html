PennyChem: Molecule Generation
Under a Synthesis Budget

Can economic constraints steer a VAE toward synthesizable drug candidates?

01 · Background

Background

Getting a drug to market costs around $2 billion and takes over ten years. A lot of that time and money disappears on compounds that looked promising on paper but turned out to be nearly impossible to make. Computational generative models have gotten pretty good at proposing novel molecules with high predicted drug-likeness, but almost all of them treat synthesis difficulty as something you check after generation, a filter, not an objective. The result is a pipeline that produces chemically interesting molecules that no one can actually make.

We wanted to see what happens when you put the cost constraint inside the model. The setup: a SMILES VAE trained on 500k drug-like molecules from ZINC20, with learned property predictors attached to the latent space. At generation time, instead of sampling randomly, we run gradient ascent on the latent vector z to maximize a reward function that directly trades off drug-likeness against synthesis cost:

R(z) = QEDpred(z) − λ · Costpred(z)

By sweeping λ from 0 to 1 and comparing four different cost proxies, SA score, SCScore, SYBA, and molecular weight, we can measure whether this approach actually steers generation in the intended direction, which metrics work best as steering signals, and what it costs in drug-likeness to improve synthesizability.

Relevant Prior Work

The SMILES VAE from Gómez-Bombarelli et al. (2018) is the architectural starting point: a VAE over SMILES strings learns a continuous latent space where properties vary smoothly and gradient-based optimization is possible. The VAE itself follows Kingma & Welling (2014), and we use cyclical KL annealing from Fu et al. (2019) to prevent the well-known posterior collapse problem in sequence VAEs. For tokenization we use the regex-based approach from Schwaller et al. (2019), which splits SMILES into chemically meaningful tokens rather than single characters.

For synthesis proxies: SA score (Ertl & Schuffenhauer, 2009) is a fragment-frequency heuristic where lower scores mean easier synthesis. SCScore (Coley et al., 2018) is trained on reaction data and estimates synthetic complexity. SYBA (Voršilák et al., 2020) is a Bayesian classifier trained to distinguish easy- from hard-to-synthesize molecules, where higher scores indicate easier synthesis. None of these have previously been used as differentiable objectives inside a generative model, they have only been used as post-hoc filters.

Hypothesis

Adding a synthesis cost penalty to latent space optimization will shift generated molecules toward lower synthesis difficulty scores in a monotone, controllable way as λ increases, and different cost proxies will differ in how efficiently they steer generation without sacrificing QED.

02 · Method

Methods

The overall pipeline is fairly simple, but we ended up running a few different experiments to tease apart what is actually driving the results. Part 1 is a basic sanity check comparing the LSTM, unconstrained VAE, and SA-constrained VAE. Part 2 is the main ablation: 4 cost metrics across 4 λ values, so 16 conditions total. Part 3 checks whether the property predictors are actually reliable enough to trust. Part 4 adds hard feasibility filtering and a combined-constraint experiment.

Dataset

We use ZINC20-Druglike: purchasable, drug-like molecules in canonical SMILES format. The VAE is trained on 500k molecules (80/10/10 split). Property predictor training uses a separate sample of 40k molecules encoded to latent vectors with the frozen VAE encoder. All property scores are computed with RDKit (QED, SA, MW) and external packages (SCScore, SYBA).

Model Architecture

Encoder

Bidirectional 2-layer LSTM

Maps SMILES token sequences to (μ, log σ²) in 128-dim latent space. Hidden dims: 256 → 512. Concatenates h and c states from both directions.

Decoder

Unidirectional 2-layer LSTM

Generates tokens autoregressively. z (128-dim) is projected into initial hidden/cell states (480-dim hidden). Teacher forcing during training; sampling at inference.

Property Heads

3-layer MLP × 5

One head per property (QED, SA, MW, SCScore, SYBA). Uses LayerNorm + SiLU activations. Inputs and targets are standardized with train-set statistics. Trained on 40k frozen latents for 100 epochs.

LSTM Baseline

Autoregressive LM, 15 epochs

Pure sequence model, no latent bottleneck, no property conditioning. Same embed/hidden dims. Serves as the upper bound on SMILES grammar quality and a no-constraint reference point.

Training Details

VAE (30 epochs): Masked cross-entropy reconstruction loss + KL divergence with cyclical annealing (10-epoch cycles). Adam, lr = 3×10−4, gradient clip 5.0. Final val loss: 0.178 (recon 0.033, KL 0.175).

Property heads (100 epochs): Encoder is frozen. MSE loss on standardized targets. AdamW, lr = 10−3, weight decay = 10−4. Separate head per property trained independently.

Constrained Generation

We sample z0 ∼ N(0, I) and run 50 steps of Adam gradient ascent (lr = 0.01) with an L2 anchor term (β = 0.01) to maximize R(z). For SYBA, which is higher-is-better, we use Cost(m) = −SYBA(m) so the framework stays consistent. For each setting we generate around 200 molecules, which is enough to get a rough sense of the trends without being too expensive computationally. We evaluate on validity (RDKit parse), uniqueness, novelty (not in a reference set of 10,001 ZINC molecules), QED, SA, SCScore, SYBA, and MW.

04 · Analysis

Analysis and Discussion

1. The unconstrained VAE actually makes synthesis worse, not better.

This one is actually a bit surprising. The VAE at λ=0 doesn’t just fail to improve synthesizability, it actively worsens it relative to the LSTM. SA goes up to 3.754, SCScore to 3.416, and SYBA drops to −0.755, meaning most generated molecules land on the wrong side of the synthesizability classifier. Optimizing for QED in latent space pulls you toward structurally complex, drug-like molecules that are harder to make. λ=0 is not a neutral baseline.

2. SA is the most efficient signal.

SA drops by 0.178 at λ=1.0 while costing only 0.009 QED. That’s a clean trade-off. It probably works this well because the predictor is reliable (R²=0.856), gradient ascent is actually pointing in the right direction rather than chasing noise. If you only pick one cost signal, SA is the one.

3. SYBA produces the biggest absolute shift.

Starting from −0.755 at λ=0, SYBA reaches +9.36 at λ=1.0. That’s the generated distribution flipping sides on the synthesizability classifier, from “most molecules probably can’t be made” to “most look feasible.” Biggest absolute shift of any metric. If you’re running a real synthesis campaign, SYBA is what you want.

4. SCScore works, just less efficiently than SA.

At λ=1.0, SCScore drops 0.118 but at a QED cost of 0.024. SA drops 0.178 at only 0.009 QED cost. That gap tracks with predictor quality, R²=0.556 vs. 0.856. A noisier predictor means gradient ascent has to push harder in latent space, disrupting other properties along the way. MW is the negative control: weight drops reliably (228.98 → 221.73 Da) but SA feasibility barely moves and slightly worsens at λ=1.0. Lighter molecules aren’t necessarily easier to synthesize.

5. The feasibility gap raises a real question: is the VAE the right architecture here?

Even at the strongest constraint (λ=1.0, SYBA), the VAE reaches 75.0% hard feasibility vs. the LSTM’s 84.5%. This gap is worth sitting with. The LSTM has no latent bottleneck, it never has to compress a molecule into 128 numbers and reconstruct it, which means it never loses the fine-grained structural information that determines synthesizability. The VAE’s 128-dim Gaussian is a significant information bottleneck, and it is plausible that the fragment-level cues that drive SA and SYBA scores are exactly the kind of structural detail that gets smoothed away in the latent compression. An alternative approach would be a flow-based model or a diffusion model, which can be made invertible and avoid the information loss entirely. Another possibility is that 500k molecules simply is not enough, the ZINC20 corpus contains hundreds of millions of purchasable compounds, and our VAE may not have seen enough variety to build a latent space that separates synthesizable from complex molecules cleanly. More training data, not a better objective, might be what is actually needed.

6. Combining constraints doesn’t simply add up.

The 4-constraint combination improves SA (3.584) and SCScore (3.331) over SA-only, but SYBA collapses from 3.614 to 0.909. MW and SYBA are pulling in conflicting directions in latent space, summing the gradients doesn’t work. Proper multi-objective optimization (NSGA-II or a learned Pareto front) is what’s actually needed here.

Bottom line: Economic constraints provide real, measurable, directional control over VAE generation, SA and SYBA work particularly well as steering signals. The VAE does not beat the LSTM on raw feasibility, and that gap likely reflects genuine architectural limitations of the bottleneck design and the scale of training data, not just a tuning problem. The VAE’s advantage is controllability: you can dial synthesis cost up and down continuously, which a pure language model cannot do. Whether that tradeoff is worth it depends entirely on what the downstream application needs.

05 · Limitations & Reflection

Limitations

Decoder quality is the main bottleneck. The VAE achieves 93–96% validity vs. the LSTM's 99.5%. The decoder has learned SMILES grammar reasonably well but not to the same fluency as a pure language model. Reinforcement learning fine-tuning on the decoder, or a larger architecture, would likely close much of that gap.

SCScore predictor needs more data. R²=0.556 is below our 0.7 reliability threshold. SCScore is trained on Reaxys reaction data and captures multi-step synthesis routes, which is harder to encode in a 128-dim latent space than fragment-frequency metrics. More predictor training molecules or a specialized architecture for route-aware properties would help.

All cost metrics are proxies, not ground truth. SA, SCScore, and SYBA are all heuristics. None of them actually performs a retrosynthetic route search. A more rigorous setup would use AiZynthFinder or ASKCOS as a scoring function, treating our proxy-based optimization as a warm start.

Was AI actually needed for this project?

Yes, but it's worth being clear about why. The main issue is that synthesis cost is a pretty awkward objective to work with. It is discrete, and there's no way to optimize it directly from a SMILES string.

What the VAE gives you is a continuous latent space where you can use learned predictors as a proxy for those signals. Without that, you are basically stuck with something like the LSTM baseline, which generates reasonable molecules but gives you no real control over what comes out. You are sampling from what it learned, and that's it.

That ability to steer generation is really the only reason to use the VAE here. If controllability is not important, a simpler model would probably do just fine.

What would we try with three more months?

A few things feel like obvious next steps. The biggest one is to stop relying on proxy scores and actually plug in retrosynthesis feedback. For example, you could run something like AiZynthFinder on generated molecules and just treat "route found or not" as a reward signal, then see how much that closes the gap with the LSTM baseline.

Another piece is the way we are handling multiple constraints. Right now it is a pretty naive combination, and it would probably make more sense to use something like NSGA-II, or train a model that is explicitly conditioned on a desired tradeoff.

And honestly, more data would likely help a lot. We were fairly limited by compute, and the decoder quality still feels like low-hanging fruit. It would be interesting to see how far scaling alone gets us, even without changing much else.

Ethics and broader implications

This project sits somewhere between computational chemistry and early-stage drug discovery, so it is worth being clear about what it is and is not. It is a proof of concept for generating molecules in a controllable way, not a tool for discovering real drugs. None of the molecules were biologically tested, and they are not meant for any kind of clinical use.

One thing worth calling out: the same approach, optimizing a latent representation toward some target property, is neutral with respect to what that property is. It could be something useful like drug-likeness, but it could just as easily be something harmful.

Tools like this are not automatically dangerous, but they are not automatically safe either. The sensible path forward is to keep developing the technology while taking safety seriously, putting thought into access, safeguards, and stress testing, rather than treating those concerns as an afterthought.
